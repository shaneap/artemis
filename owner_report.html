Where the money actually is

Analysis of point-of-sale data, 15 January – 15 July 2026
6,233 orders across 153 trading nights · $797,498 in recorded sales

The short version

Most of what we've been asking about assumes the room is full and needs managing better. It isn't. Across six months the dining room was never once at capacity, and sat a little under a third full on a typical night. Almost every question below ends in the same place: the constraint is filling seats, not turning them faster or arranging them differently.

That's worth knowing before spending money or staff attention on the wrong problem. Two of the ideas we discussed — tightening turnaround between parties, and being stricter about which party sits at which table — I tested properly, and both come out worth very little. The detail is below.

Your three questions

1

Seating parties back to back

"How to seat parties back to back if party is 2/3 guests"

Not worth chasing

A table sits empty about 30 minutes between one party paying and the next sitting down. That sounds like waste, so I checked whether it actually is, by measuring the gap against how busy the room was at that exact moment.

Median minutes a table sat empty, by how full the room was
Room wasGap
Under ¼ full65 min
¼ to ½33 min
½ to ¾27 min
Over ¾ full24 min

When the room is busy the staff already turn a table in 24 minutes. The long gaps happen on quiet nights — which means they're not a bussing problem, they're an empty-room problem. A table that turns faster only earns more if somebody is waiting for it.

Getting every turn down to that 24-minute standard is worth roughly $9,000 a year, and only in the rare moments the room is nearly full. Done the naive way — assuming a party is always waiting — the same sum comes out at $156,000, which is where this kind of number usually gets quoted from. I don't think it's real.

Value per person per 15 minutes

"Value per person per 15 mins"

Done

A guest is worth roughly $6 to $8 per 15 minutes at the table. But the more useful version is per table rather than per guest, because a table is the thing you're actually allocating:

A six-top earns more than double a two-top per hour of table time, and only stays about half an hour longer. Larger parties are the best business in the building — they're 16% of seatings and 30% of table revenue.

The catch: this tells you which parties to want, not how to seat them differently. With the room this empty there's nothing to reallocate — a two-top at a four-top displaced a larger party on about 1% of seatings. It's a marketing fact, not a floor-management one.

The other questions from your notes

—

Are the private-event rates priced right?

"$1200 for BD 3 hour reservation / $900 for E 3 hour reservation — are these priced well?"

Yes — both, comfortably

An event sells a section for three hours, so the fair comparison is what that section earns in its best three hours of ordinary trade — that's what the booking displaces.

SectionEvent rateTypical best 3 hoursBeats
Black Duck$1,200$61699% of nights

Two things worth acting on.

Midweek events are far more valuable than weekend ones. Booking Black Duck on a Tuesday nets about $830 more than the space would otherwise earn; on a Saturday it's about $458. Discounting midweek to fill the calendar costs you almost nothing and is the easiest revenue on this page.

The weekend rate is slightly low. Black Duck's best-ever three hours came to $1,440 — above the $1,200 rate. On a peak Friday or Saturday you're marginally underpricing the room.

—

Should E3 seat a party of four?

"Should E3 seat 4 when there is no party of 5/6?"

Yes — seat them

The worry is holding the big table for a large party that never shows. I measured how often that actually bites: a party of five or six turned up to find E3 occupied by a smaller group about once every five nights — and every one of those parties was still seated, because both Black Duck banquettes take that size too. Holding E3 empty costs more than it protects.

—

Should BD1 & BD2 seat a party of five?

"Same issue with BD1&2. Should they seat 5s (3+2)?"

Yes — and you already do

BD1 and BD2 are two halves of a single six-seat banquette, so a party of five fits without pushing anything together. It's already happening — 30 parties of exactly five over the six months, earning $115 per table-hour, well above a two-top. Both Black Duck banquettes sit empty through roughly half of peak hours, so there's no scarcity to ration here.

—

Reservation lengths

"Bigger tables have longer reservation… need to weight larger parties higher?"

Already handled — and the limits aren't binding

Between 43% and 56% of parties — at every size — stay past their allotted time. But the room was near-full during only 5% of those overstays, so enforcing the limit would recover almost nothing. On weighting bigger parties more heavily: the per-table-hour figures above already do exactly that, since they divide what a party spends by how long they occupy the table.

Two things you didn't ask about

The floor plan was recorded wrong, and it was distorting everything

Toast files every check against a single table code, but Black Duck and Evangeline are banquettes split across two codes each, and most Open Lounge tables pair up. So a party taking a whole banquette looked, in the data, like six people crammed onto a two-top. Roughly half of all bookings were being flagged as impossible.

I confirmed the real layout against Tock and then against the sales data independently — when one half of a banquette holds a large party, the other half is empty 92–100% of the time, exactly as it should be if they're one piece of furniture. Seat totals now come out at 22, 14, 10 and 12, matching your own figures exactly. What's left flagged is about 100 genuinely large bookings that really did span several tables.

What would help next

Every answer above points the same direction: the room isn't full, so the question worth solving is what brings people in and when. Toast can't answer that on its own — it only records the business we served, never the business we turned away or never got.

Access to the Tock booking data would close that gap. Specifically: when a booking was made, when the party was actually seated, party size, and whether it was kept, cancelled or a no-show. That tells us how far ahead people book, how many don't turn up, and which nights genuinely run out of room — none of which is visible today.

With that plus an hourly running-cost figure, I can finish the opening-hours question properly and put numbers on which nights are worth staffing.

How this was put together

Source. Toast order exports covering 15 January to 15 July 2026 — 6,233 orders after removing duplicate export rows, a retired table, and one order recording 101 guests at a bar stool.

Table time uses Toast's own opened-to-paid duration rather than the difference between opened and closed timestamps, which is inflated by end-of-night batch closeouts. Nights are counted as trading nights, so a Friday running to 1:30am counts entirely as Friday — previously that late trade was being credited to Saturday.

Not included in table figures: $85,015 of catering and private-event orders and $19,173 of takeaway. These have no table and no meaningful sitting time, so they'd distort any per-table number — but they're real revenue and are tracked separately.

Known limits. Toast records when a party paid, not when the table was cleared, so the gap between parties blends guests lingering, staff resetting, and nobody waiting. The event-pricing comparison treats the quoted rate as the whole value of the booking — if events also drive bar spend, they're worth more than shown. And the six-month window can't distinguish a seasonal dip from a real decline.
